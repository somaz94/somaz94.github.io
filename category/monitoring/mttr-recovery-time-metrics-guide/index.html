    Measuring MTTR: Four Definitions, When to Measure, and the Average Trap

    What the R stands for, where the clock starts and stops, how the choice moves availability, why averages and medians mislead, why DORA renamed the metric, and three incidents that had no start time to measure from


    Overview

    MTTR is the first number anyone reaches for when talking about incident response. Yet teams do not even agree on what the R stands for. Atlassian’s article on incident metrics notes that the R can mean repair, recovery, respond or resolve, and advises settling which MTTR you mean before you start tracking it.

    This post covers those four meanings, the points at which measurement starts and ends, MTBF and the availability arithmetic, what goes wrong when incident durations are summarized as an average, and how DORA came to drop the name MTTR.

    The last section looks at three incidents where MTTR could not be measured, because there was no way to know when the incident started. There are no measured MTTR figures for them. Every incident time and worked duration in this post is an example value made up for illustration.


    The Four Meanings of R

    The Atlassian article points out that MTTR looks like a single metric with a single meaning, but can actually be four different measurements. The four overlap, yet each has its own meaning and use.


    Name Starts Ends What the article says it is for
    Mean time to repair When repair work begins When repair and testing are done and the system is fully functional How fast the responsible team repairs. It does not measure alerting delays or the wait before repair
    Mean time to recovery (restore) When the failure happens When the system is fully back to normal The whole outage. The speed of the entire recovery process
    Mean time to respond When the first alert arrives When the system is fully back to normal Excludes the delay in the alerting system. Common in security response
    Mean time to resolve When the failure happens When the work to prevent recurrence is also done Detection, diagnosis and repair, plus prevention of recurrence


    The formula is the same for all four: add up the time within the period and divide by the number of incidents. Only the interval that goes into the numerator differs.

    The article makes one more point about resolve. This MTTR is often counted in business hours only, so if you recover at closing time and fix the underlying cause first thing the next morning, the 16 hours in between are not included. If your teams are in several locations or on-call engineers work after hours, it says, you need to decide how time will be counted.

    Besides the four MTTRs, two more metrics measure shorter intervals. MTTA (mean time to acknowledge) runs from the moment an alert fires to the moment someone starts working on it. MTTD (mean time to detect) runs from the start of impact to the moment operators become aware of the problem. The Atlassian article does not define MTTD separately, so this post follows the timeline of the Google report discussed below and uses it to mean the interval from impact start to detection.


    Measuring One Incident Four Ways

    Computing all four values for a single hypothetical incident makes the difference obvious. Every time below is made up for illustration.

    Example incident (hypothetical values)

10:12  Alert sent

10:20  Cause narrowed down, rollback started (repair begins)
10:40  Rollback complete, service normal
Next day: 2 hours spent on a prevention patch and its tests


    Metric Interval Value
    MTTD 10:00 → 10:12 12 min
    MTTA 10:12 → 10:15 3 min
    MTTR (repair) 10:20 → 10:40 20 min
    MTTR (respond) 10:12 → 10:40 28 min
    MTTR (recovery) 10:00 → 10:40 40 min
    MTTR (resolve) 40 min + 2 hours of prevention work 160 min
    Hypothetical values, made up for illustration.

    The resolve value was computed the way the Atlassian article’s example does it, by adding the time spent on prevention work to the outage time. The same incident comes out as 20 minutes or as 160 minutes depending on the definition, a factor of eight. When one team says “our MTTR is 20 minutes” and another says “2 hours 40 minutes,” the gap may come from the definition rather than from how fast either team responds.

    The table also shows why repair is the shortest. The 20 minutes between the start of impact and the start of repair drop out entirely. The Atlassian article itself says repair is not a metric for finding problems with alerting or delays before repair begins.


    When to Start and Stop Measuring

    Settling what the R means still leaves the question of where to put the timestamps. Incident Metrics in SRE (O’Reilly, 2021), written by Štěpán Davidovič of Google’s SRE team, divides an incident’s timeline into four points.


    Point The report's definition
    First product impact When the product is first seriously affected
    Detection When operators become aware of the ongoing problem
    Mitigation When the serious impact is gone, though the system may still be degraded to some extent
    Recovery When the system is fully back to normal operation. Often the same moment as mitigation, but not always


    The report defines MTTR as the mean of recovery time minus first impact time, and MTTM as the mean of mitigation time minus first impact time. Both start counting from the moment impact began.


    Start Time: Impact, Detection, Acknowledgement

    There are three candidates for the start time.

    • Impact start: when users were first affected
    • Detection: when an alert fired or someone noticed the problem
    • Acknowledgement: when the on-call engineer received the alert and started working

    Detection and acknowledgement times are recorded automatically by the alerting system and the on-call tooling. Impact start is usually set by a person after the incident, by working back through metrics and logs. That makes it tempting to use the detection time, which is already on record, as the starting point, and doing so takes the MTTD interval out of MTTR. The later the detection, the shorter MTTR looks compared with reality.

    The SLO alerting chapter of the SRE Workbook lists detection time as one of the attributes for evaluating an alert, and says long detection times can negatively impact the error budget. For users, the time before the alert fires is outage time too.


    End Time: Mitigation and Recovery

    The end time also has two candidates: when the impact was removed, by a rollback for example (mitigation), and when the system was fully back to its original configuration (recovery). If you shifted traffic to another region and user impact disappeared, but the original region is still broken, mitigation is over and recovery is not.

    The report also notes that this boundary is blurrier than it seems. If impact is gone for 90% of users and 10% are still affected, is the incident mitigated? What about 5%, or 20%? The timeline model has to settle each case as a yes or no, and the report says criticism that this classification is made subjectively and inconsistently is fair.


    Writing the Definition Down

    So before producing any MTTR number, write the definition down first. Below is an example of a template for recording it. The team fills in the parts in <>.

    MTTR definition (<> is decided by the team)

- Meaning of R: recovery
- Start: time of first user impact (settled afterwards from <metrics or logs>)
- End: one of <mitigation time / recovery time>
- Scope: <service> incidents of <severity> or higher
- Excluded: planned maintenance, incidents shorter than <N minutes>
- Time zone: UTC only
- Time outside business hours: <included / excluded>

    The exclusion rules change the number too. Davidovič also left out incidents shorter than 3 minutes or longer than 3 days when computing over the three companies’ public data. How to record each of these times in the incident record is covered in the postmortem writing guide.


    MTBF and Availability


    Time-Based and Request-Based Availability

    The Embracing Risk chapter of the SRE Book calculates availability two ways. Time-based availability is the share of time the system is up.

    Time-based availability = uptime / (uptime + downtime)

Allowed annual downtime for a 99.99% target
= 365 days x 24 hours x 60 minutes x (1 - 0.9999)
= 525,600 minutes x 0.0001
= 52.56 minutes

    That matches the figure in the book. Request-based availability is the share of requests that succeeded over a period.

    Request-based availability = successful requests / total requests

Allowed errors at 2.5 million requests a day and a 99.99% daily target
= 2,500,000 x 0.0001
= 250 requests

    The book says that for a globally distributed service like Google’s, time-based availability is usually not meaningful. Because the architecture isolates faults, some part of the world is serving some traffic at any given moment, so the service is always at least partially “up.” That is why Google defines availability in terms of request success rate. The basic idea of an SLO is touched on briefly in the post on DevOps and SRE roles.


    Availability from MTBF and MTTR

    The Atlassian article also gives a formula for availability in terms of MTBF and MTTR.

    MTBF = total uptime / number of incidents
MTTR = total downtime / number of incidents
Availability = MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR)

    With the same incident count N, the N cancels between numerator and denominator, so this formula is the same as the time-based availability formula above.

    MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR)
= (uptime / N) / (uptime / N + downtime / N)
= uptime / (uptime + downtime)

    This makes one important condition visible.

    The MTTR in this formula has to be recovery, measured over the whole downtime from impact start to recovery. Put in repair or respond, and availability is computed with part of the downtime missing.


    The names are worth sorting out once as well. The introduction to the SRE Book says reliability is a function of MTTF (mean time to failure) and MTTR. The Atlassian article separates MTTF, the average time until a non-repairable system fails, from MTBF, the average time between failures of a repairable system. For something you recover and keep using, like a service after an outage, MTBF is the right term.


    How the Choice of R Moves Availability

    Here is a calculation with example values. Over 30 days there were 2 incidents, and each took 30 minutes from impact start to recovery. In both, the alert fired 10 minutes after impact began.

    Example calculation (hypothetical values)

Period              30 days = 43,200 minutes
Total downtime      30 minutes x 2 incidents = 60 minutes
Uptime              43,200 - 60 = 43,140 minutes
MTBF                43,140 / 2 = 21,570 minutes

MTTR (recovery, from impact start)  30 minutes
Availability = 21,570 / (21,570 + 30) = 21,570 / 21,600 = 99.861%

MTTR (respond, from the alert)      30 - 10 = 20 minutes
Availability = 21,570 / (21,570 + 20) = 21,570 / 21,590 = 99.907%

Allowed downtime in 30 days for a 99.9% target = 43,200 x 0.001 = 43.2 minutes

    Computed with recovery, the real downtime of 60 minutes exceeds the 43.2 minutes allowed, and the 99.9% target was missed. Computed with respond, the two incidents count as 40 minutes of downtime, and the target looks met. The 20 minutes before the alerts fired have vanished from the number.


    Why an Average MTTR Is Hard to Trust


    One Long Incident Moves the Average

    Most incidents are short and a few are very long. In a distribution like that, the average is driven by the rare long incidents. The two quarters below are made up for illustration.


    Quarter Incident durations Mean Median
    Quarter A 9 of 10 min, 1 of 300 min 390 / 10 = 39 min 10 min
    Quarter B 10 of 15 min 150 / 10 = 15 min 15 min
    Hypothetical values, made up for illustration.


    Going by the mean, MTTR fell 61.5%, from 39 to 15 minutes. The median, though, rose 50%, from 10 to 15 minutes. The only reason the mean improved is that quarter A’s single 300-minute incident did not happen in quarter B.


    Incident Count Leaves No Trace in MTTR

    MTTR is divided by the incident count, so the count itself does not show up in the number. Suppose twice as many incidents happened, with the same distribution as quarter A.


    Quarter Incident durations Total downtime Mean
    Quarter A 9 of 10 min, 1 of 300 min 390 min 39 min
    Quarter C 18 of 10 min, 2 of 300 min 780 min 39 min
    Hypothetical values, made up for illustration.


    Downtime doubled and MTTR did not move. Davidovič’s report makes the same point, summarizing an argument from Alex Hidalgo’s Implementing Service Level Objectives: if incidents follow roughly the same distribution and only their number doubles, reliability has clearly got worse, yet the metric barely changes. The report considers this alone enough to make MTTx (metrics expressed as a mean time, such as MTTR and MTTM) a poor fit for representing the reliability of a system as a whole.


    Trends Still Swing with the Median

    So far it might seem that using the median instead of the mean would fix this. The report uses real data to show that the median is not enough either.

    Davidovič gathered incident data that three companies (each with roughly 1,000 to 2,000 employees) had published on their status pages, along with Google’s internal incident data. The three companies had 798, 350 and 2,186 incidents, of which 173, 103 and 609 were from 2019. All four data sets had a positively skewed distribution, with most incidents ending quickly, and were close in shape to a lognormal distribution.

    The report ran a Monte Carlo simulation on these distributions. The experiment assumes a product was adopted that cuts incident durations by 10%. It draws as many incidents as each company had in 2019, at random from the real distribution of durations, splits them into two groups of equal size, reduces the durations in one group by 10%, and compares the two groups’ MTTR. This was repeated 100,000 times.


    Result Company A Company B Company C
    Share of runs where MTTR looked worse despite a real 10% reduction 38% 40% 20%
    Share of runs where MTTR looked 30 or more minutes better with nothing changed 19% 23% 10%


    Even when the product really works, two to four runs in ten show MTTR getting worse, and even when it does nothing, a large improvement shows up fairly often. Even at 1,000 incidents, the 90% confidence interval of the MTTR difference between two unmodified groups contained the 10% improvement.

    The median gave the same result: at 1,000 incidents, the 90% confidence interval still contained a 10% change in the median. The p95 was swayed by the rarest and longest incidents and had even more variance. The report concludes that the problem is not the mean as a calculation, but the small number of incidents and the high variance of their durations. It adds that other incident metrics, such as time to detect, have the same problem.


    What to Look at Instead of One Number

    The report says it did not find a universal metric to replace MTTx, and recommends picking metrics to fit the question.

    The report's suggestions
    • If you improved one step of the response process, measure only that step. The report gives time to detect, or the time spent on common response tasks, as examples.
    • If the question is "has the company as a whole become more reliable," look at SLIs and SLOs that represent the reliability users experience.
    • Whatever metric you pick, first test how much it moves when nothing has changed.


    With only a handful of incidents a quarter, a list of each incident’s duration says more than a single average. For quarter A, the line “nine incidents of 10 minutes and one of 300 minutes” is more accurate than “MTTR 39 minutes,” and looking at what that 300-minute incident was tells you what to work on next quarter.


    Why DORA Renamed the Metric


    DORA’s Metrics over the Years

    DORA’s history of its metrics, published on January 2, 2026, traces how the set changed.

    timeline title DORA's software delivery metrics 2014 : First study starts from four variables : Deployment frequency, lead time for changes, MTTR, change fail rate 2015 : MTTR and change fail rate grouped as stability metrics 2023 : MTTR renamed and redefined as failed deployment recovery time 2024 : Deployment rework rate added as a fifth metric

    Milestones from DORA's history article.


    Until 2023 the metric went by “mean time to recover (MTTR)” or “time to restore service.” The reason the article gives for changing it is that the old definition did not separate failures caused by software changes from failures caused by external factors such as a data center outage. The new definition focuses only on restoring service after a production change caused a problem, and it fits the other delivery metrics better statistically. The article says the detailed reasoning is in the appendix of the Accelerate State of DevOps 2023 report.

    The current DORA metrics guide splits the five metrics into throughput and instability, and puts failed deployment recovery time on the throughput side.


    Group Metric The guide's definition
    Throughput Change lead time The time it takes for a change to go from committed to version control to deployed in production
    Throughput Deployment frequency The number of deployments over a given period, or the time between deployments
    Throughput Failed deployment recovery time The time it takes to recover from a deployment that fails and requires immediate intervention
    Instability Change fail rate The ratio of deployments that require immediate intervention following a deployment
    Instability Deployment rework rate The ratio of deployments that are unplanned but happen as a result of an incident in production


    DORA’s Number and Incident MTTR Count Different Things

    The rename also narrowed what is measured. Failed deployment recovery time counts only incidents that a deployment caused. Power outages, outages of external services, and configuration accidents unrelated to a deployment do not go into it.

    So putting the MTTR from your team’s incident records next to the performance bands in a DORA report compares two different populations. The guide explains that the metrics are best suited to measuring one application or service at a time, and that blending them across several teams or a whole organization can be problematic because of differences in context.


    Three Incidents MTTR Could Not Measure

    In Davidovič’s definition, MTTR is the mean of recovery time minus first impact time. When the first impact time or the detection time is unknown, the incident either drops out of the count altogether or goes in with a shorter duration than it really had. Going in shorter makes MTTR look better, and so does dropping out of the count, if the missing incident was longer than average.

    No measured numbers here

    The three cases below were run into or found in environments I operated myself. None of them has a measured MTTR or MTTD figure. That they could not be measured is exactly why they were chosen.


    Case 1: Prometheus Stopped and Nobody Knew

    An in-house Kubernetes cluster was monitored with kube-prometheus-stack. Then a CRD was deleted from outside the cluster, Prometheus stopped, and nobody noticed. Prometheus was the one sending the alerts, so once it stopped, nothing was left to report that it had stopped.

    An incident like this has no detection time. Prometheus, which would have sent the alert, had stopped, so there is no alert record either, and when it stopped has to be estimated later by someone working backwards. If another incident happened while monitoring was down, that incident’s impact start time is lost as well.

    So a cron watchdog on a bastion host outside the cluster was set to check every 5 minutes that the monitoring stack was alive. A CronJob inside the cluster was also considered, but ruled out because it could stop along with what it watched. The watcher has to live somewhere that does not stop together with the watched.

    kube-prometheus’s Watchdog alert works on the same principle. The alert is made to fire all the time, to confirm that the whole alerting pipeline works, and the runbook says that when it is not firing, an external system should raise the alarm.

    Below is an example showing the shape of the watchdog outside the cluster. It is not the script actually used, and the hosts and the notification path need to be changed for your environment. /-/ready on Prometheus and Alertmanager is a management API endpoint that returns 200 when each process is ready to serve requests (Prometheus, Alertmanager).

    #!/usr/bin/env bash
# Register in crontab on a 5-minute schedule: */5 * * * * /usr/local/bin/monitoring-watchdog.sh
set -u

targets=(
  "http://<your-prometheus-host>:9090/-/ready"
  "http://<your-alertmanager-host>:9093/-/ready"
)

for url in "${targets[@]}"; do
  if ! curl -fsS --max-time 10 -o /dev/null "$url"; then
    # Notify through a path that does not go through the in-cluster Alertmanager
    curl -fsS --max-time 10 -X POST -H 'Content-Type: application/json' \
      -d "{\"text\":\"monitoring watchdog: ${url} not ready\"}" \
      "<your-webhook-url>"
  fi
done

    Because the check runs every 5 minutes, the time between the monitoring stack stopping and someone knowing about it is at most about one check interval. The monitoring gap cannot be removed, but its length becomes known.


    Case 1, Continued: Several Alerts for One Incident

    In the same cluster, alert rules were split by component group and severity, and alerts of lower severity were suppressed whenever a higher-severity alert fired, so that one incident was not notified several times.

    Counting alerts as incidents breaks MTTR at the denominator. If one incident fires five alerts, it looks like five incidents, and it becomes hard to decide which alert’s time to use as the detection time. With lower-severity alerts suppressed, one incident leaves one notification and one detection time.

    In Alertmanager, inhibit_rule does this. If at least one alert matches source_matchers, alerts that match target_matchers are suppressed, and the labels listed in equal must have the same value on both alerts. The following is an example, and component_group is a label name assumed to be attached to the alert rules.

    inhibit_rules:
  - source_matchers:
      - severity="critical"
    target_matchers:
      - severity=~"warning|info"
    equal:
      - component_group

    The documentation explains that if a label listed in equal is missing from both alerts, the empty values are treated as equal and the inhibition applies. To keep alerts that lack the group label from suppressing each other, every alert rule has to carry that label without exception.


    Case 2: An Alert Gap After Splitting the Collector Tier

    On EKS, distributed tracing ran on the OpenTelemetry Collector and Grafana Tempo. Later the Collector was split into a receiving tier and a sampling tier, with routing by trace ID so that the spans of one trace always gather on the same sampling instance.

    After the split, it turned out that the existing Collector-down alert was set up in a way that would not fire even if the entire sampling tier stopped. So four new alerts were created.

    The four new alerts
    • Sampling tier down
    • Traces lost because the buffer before the sampling decision overflowed
    • Buffer approaching its threshold
    • Sampling policy evaluation errors


    Only the first of the four looks at whether the sampling tier has stopped. The other three look at the case where the process is alive but traces silently disappear, and at the state just before that. Traces are also the evidence used to find the impact start time of other incidents by working backwards. When that evidence silently goes missing, the timelines of other incidents become hard to reconstruct too.

    The existing alerts were written for a Collector with a single tier. When the structure changes, so do the failure modes the alerts have to watch for.


    Case 3: A Game Server That Returns 200 on Exceptions

    After a soft launch, real traffic started reaching a game server, but there was no numeric criterion for judging whether the service was healthy. Whether something was an incident had to be decided by a person looking at it.

    Looking at the data in order to define SLIs showed that the game server returned HTTP 200 even when an exception occurred. Of about 7.9 million request spans, 0 were recorded as errors. Request-based availability computed from this data would always look like 100%, even during an incident, and the error budget would never shrink. So the availability SLI was put on hold until the application returns errors as errors.

    From the MTTR side, this service has no signal to set an impact start time from. Failed requests go into the numerator of the request-based availability formula above as successes, so there is no point at which the error rate rises. The start of an incident becomes the moment someone judged it a problem, and that judgment varies with the person and the situation.

    Instead, signals that could be measured were made SLIs first. After confirming that request latency, taken from all spans before sampling, was actually being computed, it was recorded as a latency SLI. Candidate targets were screened against the measured distribution, removing targets like “p95 under 2 seconds” that always pass and so mean nothing.


    Questions to Ask Before Measuring MTTR

    Turning the three cases and the alert suppression into questions gives the table below.


    Question What happens to MTTR if it is missed Case
    Is there a separate way to learn that monitoring itself has stopped? No detection time is recorded Case 1
    Do several alerts collapse into one incident? The count inflates and the detection time blurs Case 1, continued
    After a structural change, do the existing alerts still see the new failure modes? Some incidents pass silently Case 2
    Are failed requests recorded as failures? Impact start cannot be set from data Case 3


    Conclusion

    MTTR is not a comparable number until the meaning of R, the start time, the end time and the incidents in scope are settled. The same incident can be 20 minutes as repair and 160 minutes as resolve, and putting a respond value into the availability formula turns a missed target into a met one.

    Even with the definition agreed, a single average makes trends hard to read. Incidents are few and their durations vary widely, so in Google’s report, a real 10% improvement quite often showed up as MTTR getting worse. The median did not solve this either. Whether reliability improved is better judged with SLIs and SLOs, and an improvement to the response process is better measured on that step alone.

    DORA, too, dropped the name MTTR in 2023 and narrowed the scope to recovery time from incidents caused by deployments. DORA’s number and a team’s incident MTTR measure different things.

    And whatever the definition, it needs a start time. When nothing reports that monitoring has stopped, when alerts stop seeing new failures after a structural change, or when failures are recorded as successes, the incident drops out of MTTR or stays on record as shorter than it was. Before reducing MTTR, make sure the fact that an incident happened, and when, gets recorded.
